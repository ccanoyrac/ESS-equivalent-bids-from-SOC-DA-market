## 1. What the paper is actually proposing

The key contribution is more radical than simply "modeling batteries using SOC."

Most electricity markets essentially ask storage owners to submit something like:

\[
\text{charge if price}<X_t,\qquad
\text{discharge if price}>Y_t
\]

for each market interval \(t\).

The problem, according to Baldick, is that \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) are not genuine physical marginal costs in the same sense as the fuel cost of a thermal generator. They are mostly **opportunity costs**, and therefore depend on what the battery thinks electricity will be worth later.

So the battery has to forecast prices before it can construct its bids. At high storage penetration this gets worse, because batteries themselves affect the prices they are trying to forecast. Baldick calls attention to this endogeneity problem.

His proposal is essentially:

> Let the ISO optimize the battery's intertemporal SOC trajectory directly.

The battery communicates its physical characteristics to the ISO:

\[
\bar p,\quad \bar g,\quad
\underline s,\quad \bar s,\quad
\alpha,\quad\beta,\quad\gamma,
\]

plus degradation costs.

But instead of submitting 24 different charge/discharge economic bids, the battery submits an economic valuation associated primarily with **where it wants to end the horizon**.

That is the genuinely interesting idea.

# 2. The mathematical core

Define

\[
p_t = \text{charging power},
\]

\[
g_t = \text{discharging power},
\]

and

\[
s_t = \text{SOC at the end of interval }t.
\]

The simplest power limits are

\[
0\le p_t\le \bar p,
\]

\[
0\le g_t\le \bar g,
\]

with

\[
p_t\perp g_t,
\]

meaning the battery should not charge and discharge simultaneously. The paper notes that this complementarity can sometimes be relaxed in a welfare-maximizing LP and will hold automatically; otherwise a binary variable can enforce it.

The SOC equation is

\[
s_t
=
\Delta T\,\alpha p_t
-
\Delta T\,\beta g_t
+
\gamma s_{t-1}.
\]

This equation is worth understanding extremely well.

Here,

\[
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}<1
\]

represents the round-trip efficiency, while

\[
0<\gamma\le1
\]

captures self-discharge.

For example, with

\[
\alpha=0.9,\qquad\beta=1,
\]

charging from the grid with \(100\) MWh results in \(90\) MWh being added to stored energy.

The paper then uses tighter SOC constraints:

\[
\gamma s_{t-1}+\Delta T\alpha p_t
\le \bar s,
\]

and

\[
\gamma s_{t-1}-\Delta T\beta g_t
\ge \underline s.
\]

These are slightly stronger computationally than simply writing

\[
\underline s\le s_t\le \bar s.
\]

# 3. The most important variable in the paper: \(\bar s_T\)

Baldick defines

\[
\bar s_T=\gamma^T s_0.
\]

This is **not an imposed terminal SOC**.

It means:

> What would the battery's SOC be at the end of the horizon if it did absolutely nothing?

If

\[
\gamma=1,
\]

then simply

\[
\bar s_T=s_0.
\]

But suppose

\[
s_0=100\text{ MWh},\qquad
\gamma=0.99,\qquad
T=24.
\]

Then

\[
\bar s_T=(0.99)^{24}(100)
\approx78.6\text{ MWh}.
\]

The terminal bid is expressed relative to this reference.

So define

\[
\Delta s_T=s_T-\bar s_T.
\]

The storage owner provides a marginal willingness-to-pay function

\[
w(\Delta s_T).
\]

And Baldick defines

\[
b(\Delta s_T)
=
\int_0^{\Delta s_T}w(z)\,dz.
\]

Since \(w\) is assumed non-increasing, \(b\) is concave.

This \(b\) is the **terminal value function**.

And there is a sentence in the paper that I think should become central to your thesis:

> In a dynamic programming interpretation, \(b\) is the value-to-go beyond the end of the market horizon.

That is where I see the biggest opportunity.

# 4. The ISO optimization problem

The storage degradation cost in the simple model is

\[
\Delta T
\sum_t
\left(
\epsilon p_t+\zeta g_t
\right).
\]

The complete market problem is conceptually

\[
\min
\left[
\text{other system operating cost}
+
\text{storage degradation cost}
-
\text{terminal storage value}
\right].
\]

More formally:

\[
\min_{x,p,g,s}
\quad
\Delta T c^\top x
+
\Delta T
\left(
\epsilon\mathbf 1^\top p
+
\zeta\mathbf 1^\top g
\right)
-
b(s_T-\bar s_T)
\]

subject to all the storage constraints, network/generator constraints, and market balance equations.

The conceptual division of responsibility is therefore:

| Information | Who provides/handles it? |
|---|---|
| Charge efficiency | ESS → ISO |
| Discharge efficiency | ESS → ISO |
| SOC limits | ESS → ISO |
| Charge/discharge limits | ESS → ISO |
| Degradation cost | ESS → ISO |
| Within-horizon optimal SOC trajectory | **ISO** |
| Within-horizon prices | **ISO clearing result** |
| Value of energy after market horizon | **ESS via \(b(\cdot)\)** |

This is the essential market-design philosophy.

---

# 5. Why this can outperform conventional bidding

Suppose the battery sees six hours ahead.

In a conventional market it has to estimate:

\[
\hat\lambda_1,\hat\lambda_2,\ldots,\hat\lambda_6
\]

and derive its charge/discharge bids from those forecasts.

If its prediction for hour 5 is wrong, it may reserve too much or too little energy in hours 1–4.

Under Baldick's formulation, the ISO knows all the other bids, system constraints, renewable forecasts, network conditions, etc., and calculates the within-horizon dispatch itself.

The battery only has to answer a different question:

> "How valuable would another MWh inside my battery be **after the end of this horizon**?"

Baldick argues that this division of forecasting responsibility is more logical.

It doesn't eliminate forecasting.

It **moves the forecast boundary**.

And that distinction is fundamental.

# 9. Research direction 1 — How should \(b(\Delta s_T)\) actually be constructed?

This is the one I would currently put at the top.

In Baldick's numerical example,

\[
w=40\$/\text{MWh}
\]

is simply assumed.

Then another case assumes

\[
w=55\$/\text{MWh}.
\]

But in a real market:

**Where does 40 come from?**

That value should depend on uncertain prices after the horizon, future renewable generation, future demand, battery SOC, expected scarcity, degradation, future reserve opportunities, and potentially many days of subsequent operation.

So the true object should resemble

\[
V_{T+1}(s_T)
=
\mathbb E
\left[
\text{optimal future profit}\mid s_T,\mathcal I_T
\right].
\]

Then

\[
b(s_T-\bar s_T)
\approx
V_{T+1}(s_T)-V_{T+1}(\bar s_T).
\]

And

\[
w(s_T)
=
\frac{\partial V_{T+1}(s_T)}{\partial s_T}.
\]

Now we have a research problem.

There is already literature using dynamic programming and ML to predict storage opportunity-value functions. For example, Zheng et al. and Baker et al. construct SOC-dependent opportunity values and turn them into storage bids.

But Baldick's market structure creates a distinctly interesting question:

> What happens if instead of translating that learned value function into **hourly charge/discharge bids**, we expose only the **terminal value function to the ISO**?

That appears to me to be the cleanest bridge between optimization, machine learning and market design.

A potential thesis question becomes:

\[
\boxed{
\text{How should terminal SOC value functions be estimated under uncertainty for SOC-based electricity markets?}
}
\]

And then compare:

\[
b^\text{perfect},
\quad
b^\text{forecast},
\quad
b^\text{learned},
\quad
b^\text{robust},
\quad
b^\text{wrong}.
\]

Against conventional interval bidding.

That could be a very substantial thesis.

# 10. Research direction 2 — How robust is Baldick's market to bad terminal forecasts?

This follows naturally from direction 1.

Baldick's main criticism of conventional storage participation is that price-forecast errors produce inefficient dispatch.

But his mechanism also contains a forecast:

\[
b(\Delta s_T).
\]

So an important scientific test is:

> Are errors in one terminal valuation actually less harmful than errors in 24 interval-by-interval bids?

This is not obvious.

We could deliberately introduce errors:

\[
\hat b=b^\star+\epsilon.
\]

Or bias:

\[
\hat w=w^\star+\delta.
\]

Or misspecification of its slope.

Then measure:

\[
\text{Welfare loss}
=
C^\text{forecast}-C^\text{perfect}.
\]

Other metrics would include storage profit, curtailment, terminal SOC, price volatility, thermal generation and regret.

This would directly test the central argument of the paper rather than simply accepting it.

That is exactly the kind of research question I like for an MSc thesis.

# 11. Research direction 3 — Day-ahead versus real-time

The main model is fundamentally DA.

Baldick only sketches RT implementation in Appendix A.4.

But the operational problem becomes much harder in RT because the battery already has:

\[
p_t^{DA},\qquad
g_t^{DA},\qquad
s_t^{DA}.
\]

Then new information arrives:

\[
\tilde W_t,\qquad
\tilde D_t,\qquad
\text{outages},\ldots
\]

and the RT market must reoptimize around those commitments.

The paper proposes using the DA SOC trajectory as the reference for RT end-of-horizon deviations.

That opens questions about:

\[
s_t^{DA}\quad\text{vs}\quad s_t^{RT},
\]

rolling-horizon effects,

forecast errors,

financial settlements,

and whether RT dispatch today destroys feasibility of later DA commitments.

The paper explicitly raises this latter prudential risk.

There is already substantial literature on multi-period pricing and DA/RT storage markets, so we would need to position the contribution carefully.

But:

> **DA–RT implementation specifically under Baldick's terminal-SOC bidding architecture**

could be a very good second layer to the thesis.

# 12. Research direction 4 — Does market power disappear, or just move?

This is a more theoretical and potentially very strong thesis.

Under the conventional framework, a strategic battery can manipulate:

\[
b_t^\text{charge},
\qquad
o_t^\text{discharge}.
\]

Under Baldick's framework these largely disappear.

But now the participant controls:

\[
b(\Delta s_T),
\]

plus reported characteristics such as

\[
\epsilon,\zeta,\alpha,\beta,\bar s,\bar p,\bar g.
\]

The paper itself notes that some of these parameters could require market-power mitigation.

Imagine a large battery knows its terminal energy is truly worth

\[
40\$/\text{MWh},
\]

but submits

\[
80\$/\text{MWh}.
\]

The ISO may preserve far more energy than socially optimal.

So a very interesting question is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Is truthful revelation of }b(\cdot)\text{ incentive compatible?}
}
\]

Almost certainly not automatically.

Then we could model a price-making battery:

\[
\max_{\hat b}
\Pi^\text{ESS}(\hat b)
\]

subject to the ISO lower-level optimization

\[
(x,p,g,s)
\in
\arg\min\{\text{market clearing using }\hat b\}.
\]

That is a bilevel problem.

There is active recent research on price-making storage and strategic bidding, including work with decision-dependent prices.

But doing it with **terminal-value bidding instead of interval bidding** would be a very interesting comparison.

# 15. I would develop the thesis in five stages

1. **Exact reproduction.** Reproduce Tables 2–4, understand every KKT condition and the origin of every reported price. We already have the primal reproduction working.

2. **Generalize the code.** Replace the six-period stylized market with arbitrary \(T\), multiple generators, multiple storage units and eventually a DC network.

3. **Implement conventional bidding as a benchmark.** This is essential. We need `Baldick SOC dispatch` versus `traditional interval bidding` under exactly the same physical system.

4. **Add uncertainty and terminal-value estimation.** Construct \(b(\cdot)\) through perfect foresight, stochastic DP, approximate DP and/or ML.

5. **Run controlled experiments.** Increase renewable penetration, storage penetration, forecast error and storage duration and determine precisely where the new design provides value.

That gives us a very clean progression from reproduction → model → contribution → evidence.

# 17. There is a particularly elegant ML/optimization angle

Suppose we have future information features

\[
X_T
=
[
\text{load forecast},
\text{wind forecast},
\text{solar forecast},
\text{calendar},
\text{fuel prices},
\ldots].
\]

We could learn

\[
\hat V_\theta(s_T,X_T)
\approx
V^\star(s_T,X_T).
\]

Then define

\[
\hat b_\theta(\Delta s_T)
=
\hat V_\theta(\bar s_T+\Delta s_T,X_T)
-
\hat V_\theta(\bar s_T,X_T).
\]

The ISO still solves a **transparent optimization model**.

The neural network is not dispatching the battery directly.

It merely estimates the future value boundary condition.

That is conceptually attractive because the architecture becomes

\[
\boxed{
\text{ML future valuation}
\longrightarrow
\text{optimization-based market clearing}
}
\]

rather than

\[
\text{black-box ML}
\longrightarrow
\text{battery action}.
\]

It keeps the physics and market constraints explicit while using learning exactly where the model is missing information about the future.

For a thesis combining electricity markets, optimization and ML, I think that is an especially promising direction.
